# Sensitivity & Bias — CQI Reference

> [!abstract] 🎯 What this document is
> A self-contained context pack for reviewing the **sensitivity** criterion of the CQI. Load it before checking revision notes, exam questions or mark schemes. It synthesises Bridgette Barrett's October 2025 Lunch-n-Learn (slides and recording) with the SME House Style Guide sensitivity page in Notion.
>
> Built with sociology and psychology in mind, but the principles and the checklist apply to every subject.

---

## The one rule that overrides everything

> [!warning] ⚖️ The specification wins
> **"Where there is a difference between the guidance in this document and the guidance in the specification, content creators should defer to the specification."**
> — SME House Style Guide (V1), Sensitivity in Content Creation
>
> SME is a revision tool. Students must meet the language they will encounter in the exam. We never substitute our preferred term for the spec's term and leave the student unprepared.

But deferring to the spec is not the end of the job. Where the spec's language is outdated, insensitive or contested, the content must **carry the spec term and signal that it is not the preferred modern term**. Two accepted mechanisms:

1. **A bracketed alternative** immediately after the spec term
2. **An Examiner Tips and Tricks callout** explaining that the exam uses term X while the currently preferred term is Y

This is the single most useful test to apply in CQI. A note that silently reproduces outdated spec terminology has a sensitivity defect. A note that uses only the modern term and hides the spec term has an accuracy/exam-readiness defect. The correct answer is almost always **both, with a signpost**.

## What sensitivity is for at SME

From the "Why Sensitivity Matters at SME" slide, and restated in the session:

- SME has a **global audience** — content must be inclusive, respectful and specification-aligned
- Many topics across subjects **require careful handling, not avoidance**
- Resources should avoid reinforcing **unconscious bias and societal bias**

> [!quote] The framing given in the session
> The objective is **not political correctness — it is maximum accessibility and empathy**.

That reframing matters for CQI. The question is never "could someone be offended?" It is: **"Does this wording put a barrier between a student and the content, or subtly teach them something untrue about a group of people?"**

---

## Why the problem exists

The "Context" slide lists the root causes. They are useful because each one predicts a different class of defect:

| Cause | Defect it produces | Where you'll find it |
|---|---|---|
| **Evolving standards** | Terms that were fine when written, now dated | Older notes, any subject |
| **Specification-driven wording** | Spec term reproduced with no caveat | Sociology, psychology, geography, history |
| **Unconscious bias** | Stereotyped examples, default-male framing, Western-centric framing | Worked examples, question stems, illustrations |
| **Time pressures** | Unchecked claims, lazy generalisations | Fast-turnaround content |
| **Limited awareness or training** | Well-meant but patronising phrasing | Everywhere |
| **Legacy content** | All of the above, unreviewed | Migrated and inherited notes |

The session opened on **unconscious bias** using the Mercator projection as the worked illustration — a representation everyone accepts as neutral that systematically distorts the relative size of the global South. The point being: bias is rarely a deliberate act. It is the default you didn't examine.

## Real feedback SME has received

These are not hypotheticals. Both were shown in the session.

> [!failure] 📩 Pearson Edexcel, endorsement review
> *"Only a small point but references to brewing, making beer, and the term 'gender' need removing."*

Two separate issues in one sentence. **Alcohol** (a PARSNiP) used as a casual illustrative context. And **'gender'** used where the spec means biological sex — a term-precision failure that reads as carelessness to a board.

> [!failure] 📩 Usersnap, user report
> *"Taiwan is a region, not a country…"*
>
> **NB:** SME had never called Taiwan a country. The complaint was factually wrong.

This second one is the more instructive. It teaches that:

- Political sensitivity generates complaints **whether or not you erred**
- The defence is **being able to show your wording against the source of truth**
- Do not reflexively "fix" content in response to a complaint without checking the original claim

## The key principles

Verbatim from the slide:

- Be accurate, empathetic and inclusive
- Avoid stereotyping, dehumanising language and overgeneralisation
- Only reference identity (e.g., gender, ethnicity, religion) when necessary
- Ensure a range of representative identities throughout whilst avoiding stereotypes
- Use fact-checked and reputable sources

> [!tip] 🔍 The "only when necessary" test
> Strike the identity marker from the sentence. Does the sentence still do its teaching job? If yes, the marker was decoration and should go. If no — if the ethnicity, gender, religion or disability is load-bearing for the concept being taught — keep it and make sure it is accurate and specific.
>
> This test is the one CQI reviewers most often forget to apply, and it catches a large share of real defects.

## PARSNiPs

The flagging acronym. When a topic touches any of these, slow down.

| Letter | Topic |
|---|---|
| **P** | Politics |
| **A** | Alcohol |
| **R** | Religion |
| **S** | Sex |
| **N** | Narcotics |
| **i** | isms (racism, sexism, ableism, ageism…) |
| **P** | Pork |

PARSNiPs is a **flag, not a ban**. Several are core specification content — narcotics in biology and psychology, sex in biology, religion in sociology and RS. The instruction is "be especially thoughtful," not "avoid."

## Language use — general guidance

From the slide, verbatim:

- Use **person-first language**: e.g., "person with epilepsy"
- Use **gender-neutral terms**: e.g., "students" not "boys and girls"
- Use **community-preferred terms**: e.g., "Black" or "Latinx"
- **Keep up with evolving terminology**; avoid outdated phrases

> [!note] 🪧 A caveat the reviewer should hold
> Person-first ("person with autism") and identity-first ("autistic person") are both defensible, and preference varies by community — the autistic and Deaf communities substantially prefer identity-first. The slide gives person-first as the default. Treat it as the default, not as a rule to enforce against a source that has good reason to differ. If the spec or a cited body uses identity-first, follow it.

## The four worked rewrites

These were the spot-the-issue exercises. They are the best single training set for what a sensitivity defect actually looks like, because in each case the original sentence reads as perfectly ordinary.

### 1. "native" and "settlers"

- **"Native"**, though preferred by some people, has negative connotations and is regarded as outdated. It has been replaced by **"Indigenous communities"**
- **"Settlers"** refers to individuals or groups who move into a territory. **"Colonisation"** is preferable, because it acknowledges that the harm came from a **systematic process involving institutions, laws and military force** — not just the arrival of people

The second point is the deep one. The word choice does not merely soften or sharpen tone; it relocates causation from individuals to systems. That is a factual claim about history, and the wrong word makes the content *inaccurate*, not just impolite.

### 2. "The boys in the class…"

Where possible, text should be **gender neutral**. → "The students in the class…"

### 3. "Old people struggle with technology."

A generalisation about a group of people; avoid. Replace with: **"Some older adults may find new technologies challenging at first."**

Note what the rewrite does: adds a quantifier (*some*), a neutral group label (*older adults*), a hedge (*may*), and a temporal bound (*at first*). Every one of those is doing work. The original is a stereotype presented as a fact.

### 4. "This revolutionary discovery was made by Western scientists."

This frames the achievement as belonging solely to one cultural or geographic group, even though many discoveries involve **global collaboration** and are often the result of **previous work which may have gone uncredited**.

> [!important] This one generalises to every STEM subject
> Attribution framing is a live sensitivity issue in physics, chemistry and biology notes, and it is almost never flagged. If a note names the discoverer, check whether the framing implies a single culture's ownership of an idea with a longer and more distributed history.

## Domain guidance

### Political topics

- **Do not ignore political issues that are in the specification**
- Always follow the specification, even where it may be uncomfortable
  - *"As a revision tool we need to use the language that students will encounter in the exam. However, we can where necessary caveat the use with preferred alternatives in brackets or the use of examiner tips."*
- Present **factually**; avoid bias
- Be careful with **territorial disputes** and **nationalistic terminology**

The House Style Guide carries an **appendix of territorial disputes** — consult it rather than adjudicating from general knowledge.

Worked example given: official government policy names that contain the word **"Indians"**. The policy name is the policy name and cannot be silently changed. The Examiner Tips and Tricks callout carries the explanation that "Indigenous peoples" is the preferred contemporary term.

### Cultural and social sensitivity

- Avoid **gender, age and race stereotypes**
- Avoid **patronising terms** — e.g. "differently abled"
- Avoid **over-generalised groupings** — e.g. "disabled people"
- **Reflect diverse identities accurately**

Worked example: **"people living with a disability"**, with reference to the **Equality Act 2010** as the definitional anchor.

### Ethnicity, race and migration

- **Be specific.** "Somali", "Japanese" — not "African", "Asian"
- Use **migrant**, **refugee**, **undocumented**
- Avoid **"illegal immigrant"** and **"alien"**
- Do not group all non-white people into a single category

Worked example: the **Edexcel A Level geography** endorsed textbook uses *"illegal immigration"*, although **the specification does not use it and no exam question has used it**. Resolution: use **"undocumented"**, and flag the outdated term either in quotation marks or via an examiner tip.

> [!tip] 🧭 Generalised rule extracted from that example
> **A term in a textbook is not a term in the spec.** Check the specification itself before concluding you are obliged to reproduce a term. In this case the obligation was imaginary — and reviewers had been reproducing the textbook's language for no reason.

### Gender and LGBTQ+ inclusivity

- Use gender-neutral phrasing by default
- Distinguish **sex** from **gender** and use whichever the specification uses, precisely — conflating them is the exact error Pearson flagged
- Reflect LGBTQ+ identities accurately where they are relevant; do not introduce them as decoration or omit them where the spec includes them

### Religion and belief

- Present beliefs and practices **factually and respectfully**, without ranking or implicit judgement
- Religious practice **changes**, and content must be kept current. The example given: the **Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee's 2024 decision** on the colour of the Nishan Sahib — a move from saffron to **basanti** — a change that instantly dates any resource that describes the flag by its old colour
- **Illustrations must never depict gods or prophets**

### Historical and economic contexts

- Avoid framings that treat one region's development path as the norm against which others are measured
- Do not present economic outcomes as though they were the natural properties of populations rather than the results of historical processes
- Terms describing national wealth are contested and evolve; check the spec, then check what the source of truth currently uses

### Illustrations and visuals

- **No depictions of gods or prophets**
- **Gestures carry meaning.** The **thumbs-up** is offensive in Greece, Sardinia and parts of West Africa
- Ensure **diverse representation across the body of content**, without tokenism and without stereotyped role assignment (the scientist, the carer, the criminal)
- Alt text is required and should describe the image, not editorialise about the people in it

## Sources of truth

Always cross-check facts against trusted references:

- **WHO**
- **UN**
- **Peer-reviewed journals**
- **BBC** and reputable news sources

And, from the House Style Guide:

- **gov.uk** — inclusive language for disability
- **Oxfam Inclusive Language Guide**

> [!danger] 🤖 Two hard rules
> - **Never use speculative or unchecked sources**
> - **Always fact-check AI-generated content**
>
> Sensitivity is precisely the domain where a language model produces confident, fluent, plausible, dated wording. Every AI-drafted passage touching a PARSNiP needs a human check against a source of truth.

## Applying this to sociology

Sociology is the highest-risk subject for the sensitivity criterion, because **its subject matter is the categories themselves**. Almost every specification point names a social group. The "only reference identity when necessary" test therefore *inverts*: identity is nearly always necessary and load-bearing, so the work shifts entirely to **precision and framing**.

What to check, in rough order of how often it goes wrong:

**Theory attribution and dated terminology.** The sociology canon is old. Functionalist, Marxist and New Right texts use language that is now unacceptable, and the specification frequently names the theory using the theorist's own term. This is the classic case for the spec-plus-caveat mechanism. Reproduce the term the exam will use; make clear it is the theorist's framing, not SME's; supply the current term.

**Framing a group as a problem.** Watch for constructions where a social group is the grammatical subject of a sentence about a deficit — underachievement, family breakdown, deviance, worklessness. Sociology specifications ask students to *explain patterns*, and the explanation is nearly always structural. A note that says "X group underachieves" has silently taught a cultural-deficit theory that the spec itself would treat as one contested position among several. Prefer: *"Sociologists have sought to explain the pattern of lower average attainment among…"* and then attribute each explanation to its school.

**Presenting a contested theory as settled.** Cultural deprivation theory, the underclass, subcultural theories of deviance — these are **positions to be evaluated**, not facts to be learned. If a revision note states them without attribution and without the counter-position, that is simultaneously a sensitivity defect and an AO2/AO3 teaching failure.

**Class, ethnicity, gender as explanatory monoliths.** "Working-class students" and "Black students" are analytical categories, not homogeneous populations. Where the spec permits, signal internal variation.

**Crime and deviance.** The single most hazardous unit. Statistics about offending by ethnicity require the accompanying point about **differential policing and reporting**, or the note teaches a falsehood. Never present recorded crime figures as offending rates without that qualification.

**Families and households.** Avoid framing any family structure as deviation from a norm. "Alternative" and "non-traditional" both encode a default.

**Religion.** Sociology of religion handles living faiths as objects of analysis. Secularisation, fundamentalism and sect/cult typologies all carry pejorative loading in ordinary usage; use them as technical terms and say so.

> [!example] 🧪 Sociology CQI probe
> For any sentence naming a social group, ask: **is a pattern being described, or is a property being ascribed?** Descriptions of patterns need a source and a date. Ascriptions of properties are almost always a defect.

## Applying this to psychology

Psychology's sensitivity risks are different in kind from sociology's. The subject matter includes **mental illness, disability, sexuality, addiction, aggression, criminality and obedience**, and much of the canonical evidence base is ethically compromised, culturally narrow, or both.

**Mental health language.** Person-first is the default: *"a person with schizophrenia"*, not *"a schizophrenic"*; *"a person who has experienced depression"*, not *"a depressive"*. Never use diagnostic terms as adjectives of ordinary behaviour ("she's a bit OCD"). Note that the specification and the DSM/ICD supply the diagnostic labels themselves — use those exactly, and build the person-first phrasing around them.

**Suicide and self-harm.** Handle with the phrasing conventions of the relevant guidance: *"died by suicide"*, not *"committed suicide"* (which retains the language of criminality). Avoid method detail entirely. This is a duty-of-care issue for a student-facing revision site, distinct from and more serious than an ordinary style point.

**Addiction, gambling and narcotics.** The House Style Guide names **gambling theory in psychology** explicitly as a topic to be handled factually — described and explained, **never normalised or glamorised**. Same for substance use. The mechanism, the reinforcement schedule, the withdrawal syndrome: fine. Anything that reads as a description of the appeal: not fine. Use *"a person with a substance use disorder"* rather than *"an addict"*; *"a person who uses drugs"* rather than *"a drug user"* where the sentence permits.

**Ethics of the classic studies.** Zimbardo, Milgram, Little Albert, Harlow, Money. These are on the specification and must be taught. Content must describe what was done to participants without euphemism, and must not narrate the studies with the relish of an anecdote. Where the specification requires evaluation, the ethical evaluation is part of the content, not an optional aside.

**Cultural bias is examinable content, not just a style concern.** Ethnocentrism, imposed etic, alpha and beta bias, and the WEIRD-sample critique are on most A Level specifications. A revision note that presents Ainsworth's Strange Situation, Kohlberg's stages or Asch's line study as culture-free findings is failing the specification *and* reproducing the bias the specification asks students to identify. Check that the note's own examples do not enact the bias the note is teaching.

**Gender.** Alpha bias, beta bias and androcentrism are, again, specification content. Watch for beta bias in SME's own worked examples — the default participant should not always be male, and studies conducted on male samples should be described as such.

**Sexuality and gender identity.** Older psychology material pathologises both. Where a historical position is on the spec, present it as historical and dated, explicitly.

**Atypical development and disability.** "Autistic person" or "person with autism" — follow the specification's own usage, and prefer identity-first where the community does, notwithstanding the general person-first default. Never "suffers from". Never "high-functioning" / "low-functioning" without qualification.

**Aggression and criminality.** Biological explanations of crime carry an obvious hazard when combined with any group category. Genetic and neural explanations must be presented with their determinism critique attached, which the spec requires anyway.

> [!example] 🧪 Psychology CQI probe
> For any study description, ask two questions: **who were the participants** (and does the note say so?), and **would the person being described recognise themselves in this sentence?**

## Applying this beyond sociology and psychology

| Subject area | Where sensitivity defects concentrate |
|---|---|
| **Biology** | Sex and reproduction; performance-enhancing drugs (named in the House Style Guide as an IGCSE biology topic to handle factually); genetics and race; disease and stigma; disability |
| **Chemistry** | Alcohol as a functional group and as a product (brewing — the exact Pearson complaint); narcotics; attribution of discoveries |
| **Physics** | Attribution and Western-centric framing of discovery; nuclear weapons contexts; default-male worked examples |
| **Geography** | Territorial disputes (consult the appendix); migration terminology; development terminology; naming of disputed places |
| **History** | Colonisation vs settlement; sourcing; naming of peoples in period sources vs modern terms |
| **Economics / business** | Development terminology; framing of poverty as a property of populations |
| **Religious studies** | Depiction rules; treating living faiths as contested objects; currency of practice |
| **Maths** | Names and contexts in worded questions; stereotyped roles; brand names |

## The CQI sensitivity checklist

Run this over any piece of content. Each item maps back to a slide, the transcript or the House Style Guide.

**Terminology**

- [ ] Does the content use the specification's exact term where the spec has one? *(Verify against the spec, not against a textbook.)*
- [ ] Where the spec term is outdated or contested, is it caveated — bracketed alternative or Examiner Tips and Tricks callout?
- [ ] Are there dated terms with no spec basis at all? *(illegal immigrant, alien, native, differently abled, the disabled, addict, schizophrenic, committed suicide)*
- [ ] Is *sex* vs *gender* used precisely and consistently?
- [ ] Is language gender-neutral where the content does not require otherwise?
- [ ] Is person-first language used, unless the community or the spec prefers identity-first?
- [ ] Are group descriptors as specific as the content allows? *(Somali, not African)*

**Framing**

- [ ] Strike each identity marker: does the sentence still teach? If yes, remove it.
- [ ] Is any group the subject of a sentence ascribing a deficit as a property?
- [ ] Is any generalisation unquantified? *(no "old people struggle with…")*
- [ ] Is a contested theory presented as settled fact?
- [ ] Is any discovery, method or idea attributed to a single culture or geography?
- [ ] Are structural causes named where causation is structural?

**PARSNiPs**

- [ ] Have I identified every PARSNiP the content touches?
- [ ] Is each handled **factually, without normalising or glamorising**?
- [ ] Is any PARSNiP present as an incidental example that could simply be replaced? *(brewing, beer, gambling as flavour text)*

**Representation**

- [ ] Across the body of content, is there a range of identities?
- [ ] Are roles in examples free of stereotyped assignment?
- [ ] Are named individuals in worked examples culturally varied?

**Images**

- [ ] No depiction of gods or prophets
- [ ] No thumbs-up gesture
- [ ] Diverse, non-tokenistic, non-stereotyped representation
- [ ] Alt text present, descriptive, non-editorialising

**Facts and sources**

- [ ] Every factual claim about a group, place or population traceable to WHO, UN, a peer-reviewed journal or a reputable news source
- [ ] Territorial and political claims checked against the House Style Guide appendix
- [ ] Currency checked — has practice, terminology or classification changed since this was written?
- [ ] All AI-generated content fact-checked
- [ ] Nothing "corrected" in response to a complaint without verifying the complaint

## How to write the flag

> [!danger] 🚫 Propose the rewrite — never make it
> A CQI is **report-only**, and that holds however offensive the wording is. Write the rewrite into the flag; do **not** write it into Cobalt. No `updateQuestion`, no `updateDocument`, no silent correction of a term on the way past.
>
> The fix belongs to the **author**. Where a tweak is small enough to be worth making directly rather than sending back, that is an **editorial call and it is Leander's alone**. Claude never makes it and never proposes making it as part of a review. A sensitivity finding that genuinely cannot wait is escalated to Leander, not actioned.

When CQI identifies a sensitivity defect, the output should be actionable, not a vibe. Structure:

1. **Quote** the exact wording
2. **Name** the defect class — dated terminology / unquantified generalisation / uncaveated spec term / attribution framing / contested theory as fact / identity marker not load-bearing / PARSNiP mishandled / unsourced claim about a group
3. **State** whether the specification constrains the wording, and cite the spec point if so
4. **Propose** the rewrite, including the caveat mechanism if the spec term must stay
5. **Rate** severity: does it mislead the student factually, or is it tone?

> [!success] ✅ Model flag
> **Quote:** "The Edexcel spec requires students to evaluate the causes of illegal immigration."
> **Defect:** Dated terminology with no spec basis.
> **Spec position:** The specification does not use "illegal immigration"; no exam question has used it. The term comes from the endorsed textbook.
> **Rewrite:** "…the causes of undocumented migration."
> **Severity:** Medium. Not a factual error for the student, but the term is dehumanising and SME is under no obligation to reproduce it.
